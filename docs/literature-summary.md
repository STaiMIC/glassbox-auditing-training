# Background Literature Review: Variant Scoring, Explainability, Structured Outputs, and Workflow Provenance

GlassBox Deterministic Rule-Based Triage for Post-VCF Workflow Testing - Systems Medicine

> **Scope note:** This literature review discusses computational variant scoring, explainability, structured outputs, and workflow provenance because these areas motivate the longer-term design space for this repository. The current repository implementation uses placeholder, deterministic, rule-based triage logic for reproducibility testing only. It is not a validated clinical method and must not be used to prioritise real clinical variants.

The following review summarises background research across four domains that may inform future work around the GlassBox project: computational support for variant interpretation, explainability for genomic evidence review, reliability of structured outputs, and workflow provenance tracking.

## 1. Computational Support for Variant Interpretation

Ioannidis et al. introduced REVEL, Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner, a random-forest ensemble combining thirteen individual pathogenicity prediction tools, including SIFT, PolyPhen-2, FATHMM, VEST, MutPred, and eight conservation scores, to score missense variants on a 0-1 scale (1). Trained on recently discovered disease variants and rare neutral exome variants, REVEL outperformed seven competing ensemble methods, MetaSVM, MetaLR, CADD, DANN, Condel, KGGSeq, and Eigen, across two independent test sets, achieving AUC improvements of 0.027-0.143 over the next-best method (1). Its strongest advantage was on rare variants with allele frequencies below 0.5%, a category commonly encountered in NGS-based workflows (1). Pre-computed scores for all possible human missense variants are publicly available, making REVEL relevant background for possible future post-pipeline annotation or review workflows (1).

Cheng et al. published AlphaMissense, a deep-learning model fine-tuned from AlphaFold on human and primate population-frequency data, producing pathogenicity scores for all 71 million possible single amino-acid substitutions in the human proteome (2). By integrating structural context, protein language modelling, and weak labels derived from population frequency rather than human-curated annotations, AlphaMissense avoids some circularity biases that affect tools trained directly on clinical databases (2). It achieved an auROC of 0.940 on a class-balanced ClinVar test set, surpassing the prior best non-ClinVar-trained model, EVE, auROC 0.911, and confidently classified 89% of all missense variants as either likely pathogenic or likely benign at 90% precision (2). These pre-computed predictions are relevant to the broader design space for future variant interpretation workflows, but they are not implemented in the current triage logic in this repository.

De Paoli et al. introduced VarChat, a generative literature-retrieval tool that retrieves and summarises the published literature associated with a queried variant, returning a concise summary with ranked PubMed references and linked ClinVar records (3). VarChat demonstrates that, even where computational scores exist, natural-language synthesis may help users inspect supporting evidence more efficiently (3). The paper also documents the risk of unsupported generated statements and stresses that outputs must be clearly grounded in verifiable sources. This concern motivates the auditability pattern used in this repository, although the current implementation does not deploy VarChat or any other generative system.

## 2. Explainability for Genomic Evidence Review

Abe et al. proposed an explainability framework for variant pathogenicity estimation that combines a high-performance deep-learning engine, Deep Tensor, with a large-scale knowledge graph integrating ClinVar, dbNSFP, COSMIC, and dbscSNV, totalling more than 15 billion RDF triples (4). Deep Tensor achieved an AUC of 0.98, outperforming both random forests, AUC 0.97, and decision trees, AUC 0.90, while also generating natural-language explanatory sentences structured around ACMG classification criteria (4). Each sentence corresponds to a distinct, independently verifiable evidence item, such as a PROVEAN score, a neighbouring pathogenic variant in ClinVar, or a COSMIC entry, rather than requiring all conditions to be satisfied simultaneously as in a decision tree (4).

The authors identify two challenges relevant to future explainable genomics tools: combining high accuracy with genuine interpretability, and ensuring explanations are legible to domain reviewers rather than only to tool developers (4). The current GlassBox repository does not implement this type of model. Instead, it uses a deterministic, rule-based placeholder triage step with structured output validation and audit-bundle generation. This design allows future components to be evaluated against a reproducibility and provenance layer without changing the basic workflow architecture.

## 3. Reliability and Consistency of Structured Outputs

Although the current prototype uses deterministic rule-based logic, the subworkflow is designed around structured outputs so that future components could be evaluated safely and reproducibly. This design choice is motivated by literature showing that structured extraction systems can be prone to run-to-run instability and format failures.

Neveditsin et al. compared JSON, YAML, and XML serialisation for structured extraction from clinical notes across multiple small language models, finding that JSON consistently yielded the highest parseability, although robustness still declined with longer documents and certain note types (5). Constrained-decoding approaches enforce schema conformance directly during generation rather than relying on prompting alone. These approaches build automata from the target grammar so that only schema-valid tokens can be sampled at each generation step, providing a technical basis for treating schema validation as a core workflow component rather than an optional afterthought (6).

Tam et al. further found that restricting language models to structured formats can reduce reasoning accuracy by 10-15% relative to free-form generation, suggesting that any future model-based extension should be benchmarked for both output stability and reasoning quality, not stability alone (7). This literature does not describe the behaviour of the current repository implementation. It explains why `JSON_VALIDATION` and `AUDIT_BUNDLE` are included as first-class, pluggable workflow components before any future model-based component is introduced.

## 4. Workflow Provenance Tracking

Leo et al. presented Workflow Run RO-Crate, WRROC, an extension of the RO-Crate packaging standard that captures both prospective provenance, meaning the workflow plan, and retrospective provenance, meaning what actually happened during execution, at configurable levels of granularity (8). Three nested profiles serve different use cases: Process Run Crate for individual tool executions, Workflow Run Crate for complete managed workflow runs, and Provenance Run Crate for fine-grained step-level detail (8).

At the time of publication, WRROC had been implemented in six workflow management systems: Galaxy, COMPSs, StreamFlow, WfExS, Sapporo, and Autosubmit, demonstrating broad applicability across heterogeneous environments (8). The authors demonstrated that WRROC-packaged provenance is sufficient to automatically re-execute a workflow from its archived record. The same digital pathology CWL workflow was successfully reproduced from provenance records produced by two different execution engines, cwltool via runcrate and StreamFlow, yielding interoperable outputs (8). The SPARQL-queryable RDF representation also allows downstream users to programmatically trace outputs back to the specific tool version and input that produced them (8).

For the current GlassBox repository, provenance tracking is implemented at a lightweight workflow level. The repository uses a committed test VCF, deterministic rule-based triage, structured JSON output validation, and an audit bundle to demonstrate a reproducible pattern. The committed test VCF should be described only as a committed test VCF. 
## Summary

The literature reviewed above motivates reproducible and auditable post-pipeline workflow design. However, the current repository does not implement a validated clinical method, does not perform clinical prioritisation, and does not deploy a model-based triage system. Its current purpose is narrower: to demonstrate a deterministic, rule-based reproducibility pattern using a committed test VCF, structured validation, and workflow audit outputs.
